\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) depends on retrieved context, but common evaluations
confound retrieval error with generator behavior. This paper introduces a counterfactual
benchmark that changes a retrieved passage while holding the question, answer, and prompt
fixed. The benchmark applies entity substitution, factual negation, and meaning-preserving
paraphrase to 1{,}000 SQuAD v1.1 question-answer pairs. Six language models, four
open-weight and two closed-source, are evaluated in every condition ($N=24{,}000$
model-condition inputs). Relative to original context, mean NLI faithfulness on corrupted
context decreases by 0.153--0.295 across models, and abstention rises by 13.4--30.9
percentage points; paired 95\% confidence intervals exclude zero for every primary
comparison. Hallucination is observed in 0--9\% of outputs, with some zero-event cells
having nonzero uncertainty. The dominant failure is therefore neither uniformly faithful
following nor pervasive fabrication, but insufficiently selective abstention. The two
closed-source models have the largest abstention-cliff scores (0.241 and 0.244), while
the two Llama models have the smallest (0.173 and 0.192). A matched, 200-example
similarity-score ablation lowers a descriptive abstention-calibration discrepancy but also
raises abstention on clean context, so it does not improve selective abstention. Human
validation of 80 perturbations identifies predictable weaknesses in rule-based negation
and entity substitution. The benchmark isolates a concrete retrieval-generation failure
mode and provides evidence that calibration depends more on model family than parameter
count in this setting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become the dominant architecture for grounding
large language model (LLM) outputs in external knowledge~\cite{lewis2020rag}. By coupling
a dense retriever with a generative model, RAG systems reduce hallucination and enable
up-to-date answers without retraining. They are now widely deployed in enterprise search,
legal document analysis, medical question answering, and customer-facing AI assistants.
Retrieval is inherently noisy: retrievers regularly surface stale documents,
near-duplicate passages with incorrect dates or entities, or topically adjacent but
factually mismatched chunks. Retrieval failure is not an edge case but a routine feature
of production RAG systems.

Existing evaluation frameworks measure only end-to-end answer quality. RAGAS~\cite{es2023ragas}
and ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2023ares} score faithfulness and answer relevance, but both
assume the retrieved context is the \emph{best available} context and treat errors as a
monolithic signal. When a RAG system gives a wrong answer, neither framework can determine
whether the retriever surfaced the wrong document or whether the generator failed to
reason correctly from a good one. TruLens and the TREC RAG Track~\cite{trec2024rag}
similarly measure pipeline outputs without isolating fault.

That attribution gap prevents system designers from determining whether retrieval or
generation warrants intervention. It also leaves model developers without a benchmark
for the retrieval-generation interface under controlled failure conditions.

The benchmark addresses this gap by systematically modifying retrieved context in three
ways and measuring the resulting generator response. Because the modification is known,
the source of a context error is controlled. Four context conditions are defined for each
question-answer pair:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Original}: unmodified gold context (baseline)
  \item \textbf{Paraphrase}: surface rewording with facts preserved (control)
  \item \textbf{Entity swap}: one named entity replaced with a plausible same-type alternative
  \item \textbf{Negation}: core factual claim directly contradicted via dependency-parse negation
\end{itemize}

The paraphrase condition serves as a control: a robust model should perform identically on
paraphrase and original. Meaningful divergence on entity swap and negation reveals failure
modes of practical interest.

This paper addresses four research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item Do LLMs faithfully follow corrupted context, even when it is wrong?
  \item Do LLMs hallucinate when retrieved context conflicts with ground truth?
  \item Do LLMs appropriately abstain (``I don't know'') under corrupted retrieval?
  \item Does model scale, family, or open- vs.\ closed-source status predict better
        graceful degradation?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item A controlled counterfactual RAG benchmark built on SQuAD
        v1.1~\cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, with 1{,}000 paired examples and four context
        conditions per model.
  \item An evaluation of six models spanning open-weight and closed-source families,
        with paired confidence intervals and corrected paired tests for the primary
        comparisons.
  \item A failure-mode taxonomy distinguishing three generator archetypes:
        \emph{context-follower}, \emph{hallucinator}, and \emph{over-abstainer}, with
        evidence that model family is more predictive of abstention calibration than
        parameter count in the evaluated set.
  \item A matched similarity-score ablation and human validation of 80 perturbations,
        which qualify the scope of the empirical findings.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. \Cref{sec:related} reviews related
work. \Cref{sec:benchmark} describes the benchmark design. \Cref{sec:pipeline} details
the evaluation pipeline. \Cref{sec:results} presents results. \Cref{sec:analysis}
provides analysis and the failure-mode taxonomy. \Cref{sec:discussion} discusses
implications. \Cref{sec:limitations} states limitations. \Cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{RAG evaluation.}
RAGAS~\cite{es2023ragas} and ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2023ares} score faithfulness and answer
relevance using LLM judges, and the TREC RAG 2024 Track~\cite{trec2024rag} provides
large-scale information-retrieval-style evaluation. These frameworks evaluate the final
answer and retrieved context together, without holding retrieval quality constant. The
present benchmark instead varies only the \emph{content} of retrieved context, supporting
attribution of behavioral differences to the generator.

\paragraph{Robustness to adversarial context.}
Prior work on adversarial reading comprehension includes AddSent~\cite{jia2017adversarial},
which appends distracting sentences to SQuAD passages, contrast sets via minimal manual
perturbations~\cite{gardner2020contrast}, and counterfactual data
augmentation~\cite{kaushik2020counterfactual}. The present benchmark targets a different
problem: it simulates plausible wrong entities and contradicting documents, then measures
abstention calibration, a behavior not emphasized by prior robustness benchmarks.

\paragraph{Hallucination, abstention, and irrelevant context.}
TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa} measures false statements on misconception-prone
questions; calibration literature~\cite{kadavath2022language, xiong2024can} studies
whether model confidence correlates with accuracy; and selective
prediction~\cite{geifman2017selective} formalizes abstaining when confidence is low. Most
relevant to this setting, Shi et al.~\cite{shi2023large} show that irrelevant context can
mislead LLMs even when correct context is also present. This benchmark treats abstention
as a first-class metric under a controlled perturbation protocol and connects abstention
rates to specific retrieval failure modes. CheckList~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}
and ANLI~\cite{nie2020anli} establish the broader paradigm of behavioral testing via
perturbations; this benchmark applies similar logic but centers on abstention calibration
and production RAG failure modes.

\paragraph{Open-weight vs.\ closed-source model behavior.}
Recent work compares open-weight and closed-source LLMs on reliability and calibration
tasks~\cite{kadavath2022language, xiong2024can}. The mechanisms underlying observed
differences, including instruction tuning, preference optimization, and training-data
composition, remain uncertain. By holding the prompt, task, and evaluation protocol
constant, the present study measures abstention calibration as one axis of difference
between the evaluated model families.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Seed Dataset}

Seed data come from SQuAD v1.1~\cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, a reading-comprehension
dataset of Wikipedia passages paired with crowd-sourced questions and extractive answers.
The following filters obtain clean, unambiguous examples:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item The gold context must be extractable as a single sentence containing the gold answer
        (the sentence is found by splitting on periods and selecting the first that
        includes the answer string verbatim; sentences shorter than 20 characters are skipped).
  \item The gold answer string must appear verbatim in the selected context sentence.
\end{itemize}

Filtering yields 1{,}000 question-answer pairs, sampled randomly from the full SQuAD
v1.1 training split. Each example has the format:
\begin{center}
\texttt{\{id, question, gold\_answer, gold\_context\}}
\end{center}
\Cref{tab:dataset-stats} reports dataset statistics.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Dataset statistics for the 1{,}000-example evaluation split.}
\label{tab:dataset-stats}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Total QA pairs                     & 1{,}000 \\
Source                             & SQuAD v1.1 (Wikipedia) \\
Avg.\ gold context length (tokens) & 27.2 \\
Avg.\ question length (tokens)     & 10.0 \\
\bottomrule

\end{table}

Answer-type labels (person, organization, location, date/number) were derived during
error analysis using named-entity recognition applied to gold answers. They are reported
in \Cref{sec:crosstab} and were not used to construct the base dataset.

\subsection{Perturbation Types}

Four context variants are generated for each question-answer pair. \Cref{tab:perturbations}
summarizes the conditions.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Perturbation conditions. Each QA pair is evaluated under all four conditions.}
\label{tab:perturbations}
\begin{tabular}{llccL{3.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Transformation} & \textbf{Facts} & \textbf{Wording} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
Original    & None                        & $\checkmark$ & $\checkmark$ & Baseline / upper bound \\
Paraphrase  & LLM surface rewrite         & $\checkmark$ & $\times$     & Control: surface sensitivity \\
Entity swap & Same-type NER replacement   & $\times$     & $\checkmark$ & Simulates wrong-entity retrieval \\
Negation    & Dependency-parse negation   & $\times$     & $\checkmark$ & Simulates contradicting retrieval \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Original.} The unmodified gold context sentence. It sets the upper bound for
correctness and the expected lower bound for abstention: a well-calibrated model should
rarely decline to answer when the context is correct and directly relevant.

\paragraph{Paraphrase.} A language model rewrites the gold context sentence while
preserving factual content. A robust model should produce similar values for paraphrase
and original context. Divergence indicates sensitivity to surface wording rather than
semantic content.

\paragraph{Entity swap.} Named entities in the context are identified, and one entity
with a supported type is replaced by a plausible alternative of the same type. For
example, a person is replaced by another person and a location by another location. This
condition simulates a retrieved document that retains the claim structure but contains a
wrong key fact.

\paragraph{Negation.} A rule-based transformation negates the central predicate of the
gold context sentence while preserving the remaining content. This simulates a retrieved
record or policy that directly contradicts the original claim.

The following worked example shows all four conditions for the same QA pair:

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Who co-founded Microsoft?\\[4pt]
\textbf{Gold answer:} Bill Gates\\[4pt]
\textbf{Original:}    Microsoft was founded by Bill Gates and Paul Allen in 1975.\\
\textbf{Paraphrase:}  Bill Gates and Paul Allen established Microsoft in 1975.\\
\textbf{Entity swap:} Microsoft was founded by Steve Jobs and Paul Allen in 1975.\\
\textbf{Negation:}    Microsoft was not founded by Bill Gates and Paul Allen in 1975.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{Perturbation Quality}

Automatic transformations can introduce fluency errors or incoherent outputs, especially
when negating complex sentences. The negation procedure handles auxiliary and simple-verb
constructions, with a conservative fallback for other sentences. Entity-swap examples are
created only when a named-entity type has a plausible replacement.

To validate pipeline quality, two independent annotators rated 80 randomly sampled
examples (20 per condition) on two dimensions: fluency (1 = grammatically broken,
2 = slightly awkward, 3 = fluent and natural) and correctness (1 = perturbation failed,
2 = partial success, 3 = clear success). Inter-rater agreement was Cohen's
$\kappa = 0.396$ for fluency and $\kappa = 0.479$ for correctness. Mean fluency
across all conditions was 2.725 and mean correctness was 2.475.
Entity-swap errors concentrated in chemical compounds and domain-specific names.
Paraphrase errors primarily altered quoted speech. Negation errors were most common in
multi-clause sentences, where negating a central predicate could create a contradiction
with a dependent clause. \Cref{sec:limitations} discusses the implications of these
quality limits.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Retriever}

For each question-answer pair and context condition, a dense retriever supplies one
passage ($k=1$), using normalized Sentence-BERT embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}.
The retrieved passage is the corresponding perturbed sentence. Consequently, every model
receives exactly the intended context, and differences across conditions reflect response
to content changes rather than variation in candidate retrieval.

\subsection{Generator}

Six models spanning open-weight and closed-source families are evaluated at temperature
zero with the same prompt template.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{llama3.2:3b} (3B parameters): smallest open-weight model
  \item \texttt{llama3.1:8b} (8B parameters): same family at larger scale
  \item \texttt{mistral:7b}  (7B parameters): different instruction-tuning lineage
  \item \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}  (7B parameters): non-English-centric training corpus
  \item \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}: closed-source, OpenAI instruction-tuning lineage; accessed via OpenAI API
  \item \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}: closed-source, Anthropic instruction-tuning lineage
\end{itemize}

All models are prompted at temperature 0 for reproducibility using a system prompt and
a structured user message:

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{System:} You are a precise question-answering assistant. Answer the question
using ONLY the information provided in the context. If the context does not contain
enough information to answer the question, respond with exactly: I don't know. Do not
add information from your own knowledge. Keep your answer concise; one sentence or
less.\\[6pt]
\textbf{User:}\\
\texttt{Context:}\\
\texttt{[1] \{retrieved\_context\}}\\[4pt]
\texttt{Question: \{question\}}\\[4pt]
\texttt{Answer:}
\end{examplebox}

\noindent The prompt is identical for all six models, so observed differences in
abstention and faithfulness cannot be attributed to prompt wording.

\subsection{Metrics}

\Cref{tab:metrics} defines all six evaluation metrics.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Evaluation metrics and desired direction on corrupted conditions. Formal
definitions appear below.}
\label{tab:metrics}
\begin{tabular}{L{2.7cm}L{9.5cm}L{1.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Operationalization} & \textbf{Desired} \\
\midrule
Faithfulness       & NLI entailment of the answer by retrieved context & Low \\
Exact match (EM)   & Normalized match with the gold answer           & Low \\
Token F1           & Token-overlap F1 with the gold answer           & Low \\
Hallucination      & NLI-contradictory answer with low gold-answer overlap & Low \\
Abstention rate    & Output starts with ``I don't know''             & High \\
Contradiction rate & NLI contradiction between answer and retrieved context & High \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For example $i$, let $a_i$ denote a generated answer, $c_i$ the retrieved context,
$g_i$ the gold answer, $E_i$ the NLI entailment probability for $(c_i,a_i)$, and $K_i$
the NLI contradiction indicator for $(c_i,a_i)$. Let $M_i$ and $F_i$ denote normalized
exact match and token F1 against $g_i$, respectively. The reported condition-level
metrics are means over the $n$ examples. Faithfulness is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Faithfulness} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} E_i,
  \quad E_i = P_\text{NLI}(\text{entailment}\mid c_i,a_i).
  \label{eq:faithfulness}
\end{equation}
The NLI model is \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base}. Exact match and token F1 are
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{EM}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}[M_i=1],
  \qquad
  \operatorname{F1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_i.
  \label{eq:answer-quality}
\end{equation}
An answer is counted as a hallucination only when it contradicts the retrieved context,
has little overlap with the gold answer, and the answer itself contradicts the gold answer:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Hallucination} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \mathbb{1}[K_i=1\ \land\ F_i<0.2\ \land\ \operatorname{NLIContradict}(g_i,a_i)=1].
  \label{eq:hallucination}
\end{equation}
With $A_i=\mathbb{1}[a_i\text{ begins with ``I don't know''}]$, abstention and
contradiction are
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Abstain}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_i,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Contradiction}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_i.
  \label{eq:abstention-contradiction}
\end{equation}

The abstention-cliff score summarizes condition sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_\text{abstain} = \frac{1}{2}
    \bigl[\text{Abstain}(\text{entity\_swap}) + \text{Abstain}(\text{negation})\bigr]
    - \text{Abstain}(\text{original})
  \label{eq:cliff}
\end{equation}
A higher $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ indicates more abstention on corrupted than clean context.
An ideal model would have a large positive score by abstaining on corrupted inputs while
answering clean inputs.

The similarity-score ablation also reports an abstention-calibration discrepancy, adapted
from expected calibration error. Partitioning outputs into ten bins $b$ by $E_i$, with
$\bar{A}_b$ and $\bar{E}_b$ denoting mean abstention and faithfulness in each bin, yields
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{ECE}_{\mathrm{abstain}} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{n}
  \left|\bar{A}_b-(1-\bar{E}_b)\right|.
  \label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
This descriptive measure is distinct from the standard probability-calibration ECE because
faithfulness, rather than a predicted probability, defines the target abstention rate.

\subsection{Statistical Testing}

All primary comparisons are paired because each question-answer pair appears in every
condition. For a paired difference $d_i$, the paper reports the mean difference and a
two-sided 95\% confidence interval,
\begin{equation}
  \bar d \pm 1.96\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}.
  \label{eq:paired-ci}
\end{equation}
This interval is used for continuous faithfulness and binary-rate differences at
$n=1{,}000$. Abstention changes are additionally tested with exact two-sided McNemar
tests. The 12 pre-specified original-versus-corrupted abstention tests are adjusted with
the Holm procedure. Paraphrase comparisons are diagnostic and reported with confidence
intervals rather than used to support the primary claims.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Paired mean difference and 95\% confidence interval} for faithfulness,
        EM, F1, hallucination, and contradiction.
  \item \textbf{Exact McNemar test} for the primary binary abstention comparisons.
  \item \textbf{Holm correction} across the 12 primary abstention tests.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ1: Do Models Follow Corrupted Context?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Faithfulness decreases from original to corrupted conditions across all six models. The
paired 95\% confidence intervals in \Cref{tab:paired-effects} exclude zero in every
primary comparison. \texttt{llama3.1:8b} has the highest baseline faithfulness (0.79)
and decreases to 0.62 on entity swap and 0.55 on negation.
\texttt{mistral:7b} starts lower (0.67) and falls most steeply to 0.40 on negation
suggesting less tendency to follow context under contradiction. Among
closed-source models, \texttt{claude-haiku} shows the lowest baseline EM (0.06) and F1
(0.32) on original context, indicating a more conservative answering style even on clean
input.

Paraphrase effects are smaller than corruption effects but are not negligible. Across
models, the paired faithfulness change is $-0.034$ to $-0.051$, compared with $-0.153$
to $-0.295$ for the corrupted conditions. EM also falls by 2.6--9.9 percentage points.
This control therefore reveals surface-form sensitivity, and it should not be treated as
an identical-to-original condition. The substantially larger effects of entity swap and
negation remain the central result. Full diagnostics appear in
\Cref{tab:paraphrase-diagnostic}.

\subsection{RQ2: Do Models Hallucinate?}
\label{sec:rq2}

Hallucination rates are low across all six models and conditions, ranging from no observed
events to 9\%. Entity swap produces the highest observed rate for every model:
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} reaches 9\%, and \texttt{llama3.1:8b} and \texttt{mistral:7b} each
reach 7\%. A plausible wrong entity may therefore be more likely to elicit confabulation
than an explicit negation. Zero-event cells should be interpreted cautiously: with
$n=1{,}000$, the exact one-sided 95\% upper confidence bound for a zero observed rate is
0.37\%, rather than zero.

\texttt{claude-haiku} has the lowest observed hallucination rates, from no observed events
to 2\%. The data do not establish the cause of this difference. Across all models, the
larger and more consistent behavioral change is increased abstention rather than
confabulation.

\subsection{RQ3: Is Abstention Calibrated?}
\label{sec:rq3}

The abstention results are central to the benchmark. A well-calibrated model would show
low abstention on original and paraphrase conditions and high abstention on corrupted
conditions. Every primary paired comparison increases abstention, with 95\% confidence
intervals that exclude zero and Holm-adjusted $q<.0001$.

The Llama family shows the weakest calibration. \texttt{llama3.2:3b} abstains at only
14\% on correct original context and reaches 30\% on entity swap and 32\% on negation
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.173$). \texttt{llama3.1:8b} shows a similarly
flat response (baseline 10\%, rising to 28\% and 31\% on corrupted conditions,
$\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.192$). Both models commit readily on clean context but fail
to substantially increase abstention under corruption.

Among open-weight models, \texttt{mistral:7b} and \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} show stronger
calibration. \texttt{mistral:7b} rises from 13\% baseline to 43\% on negation, a 30
percentage-point increase, though entity swap abstention rises only modestly to 26\%,
reflecting the asymmetric failure mode described in \Cref{sec:mistral}.
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} shows a more balanced response: 37\% on entity swap and 43\% on
negation ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.234$).

Closed-source models achieve the strongest abstention cliffs. \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} rises
from 14\% baseline to 32\% on entity swap and 44\% on negation
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.241$). \texttt{claude-haiku} rises from 16\% to 35\% and
46\% respectively ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.244$), the highest of any model evaluated.
Both closed-source models show more balanced detection across entity swap and negation
than their open-weight counterparts.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Failure Mode Taxonomy}

The joint distribution of faithfulness, EM, hallucination, and abstention motivates three
descriptive generator archetypes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Context-follower.} High faithfulness on corrupted conditions, low EM. The
    model accepts the retrieved context even when it is wrong and produces answers
    consistent with the incorrect passage. Errors of this type are difficult to detect in
    production because the outputs are coherent and internally consistent. No evaluated
    model is a pure context-follower, but all show partial context-following on entity
    swap (faithfulness 0.51--0.62 across open-weight models).

  \item \textbf{Hallucinator.} Low faithfulness, low EM, elevated hallucination rate. The
    model disregards the retrieved context and produces a plausible-sounding answer from
    parametric memory. This behavior is uncommon in the observed data: hallucination
    peaks at 9\%. \texttt{claude-haiku} has the lowest observed rate, from no observed
    events to 2\% across conditions.

  \item \textbf{Over-abstainer.} High uniform abstention regardless of context quality.
    The model declines to answer at similar rates on clean and corrupted context alike.
    All six models show some degree of over-abstainer behavior. The Llama family shows it
    most clearly: \texttt{llama3.2:3b} abstains at 14\% on correct original context and
    produces a cliff score of only $\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.173$, the weakest of any
    model evaluated.
\end{itemize}

An ideal model would commit on clean context and abstain on corrupted context, producing a
large positive cliff score. The observed scores range from $+0.173$ to $+0.244$. All six
models therefore show room for more selective abstention, but this sample does not support
a claim about all current LLM families.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/fig4_taxonomy_scatter.pdf}
\caption{Failure mode taxonomy. Each point is a (model, condition) pair plotted by
  faithfulness rate ($x$) and abstention rate ($y$) across all six models. Shaded
  regions mark the three archetypes. Corrupted conditions (entity swap, negation) move
  left and up relative to original/paraphrase, but many remain in the over-abstainer
  region rather than the ideal (low faithfulness, high abstention) region.}
\label{fig:taxonomy}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Mistral's Asymmetric Response to Failure Modes}
\label{sec:mistral}

\texttt{mistral:7b} shows an asymmetric response across corruption types. It abstains at
only 26\% on entity swap (barely above its 13\% baseline) but rises to 43\% on negation,
a 30 percentage-point increase. Mistral is particularly sensitive to explicit logical
contradiction but is less responsive to entity-level substitutions. Direct factual
contradictions therefore produce a larger abstention change than plausible wrong entities
in this model.

\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} shows a more balanced response: 37\% on entity swap and 43\% on
negation. Its observed cliff score is the largest among the four open-weight models,
although both closed-source models have higher scores in this six-model comparison.

\subsection{Answer-Type Cross-Tabulation}
\label{sec:crosstab}

The expanded dataset enables analysis of failure mode distributions across answer types.
Drawing on the error analysis output across all six models, three patterns emerge.

Organization-type answers show the highest hallucination rates on entity swap across
models. This is consistent with the hypothesis that organization names are well-represented
in parametric memory: when a retrieved org name is wrong, models are more likely to
substitute a known alternative from training data rather than abstaining or following the
corrupted context.

Location-type answers show the lowest abstention rates on entity swap despite corruption.
Models therefore more often accept and echo a plausible wrong location than abstain. A
wrong city or country name is structurally indistinguishable from a correct one without
world knowledge, which may make location swaps difficult to detect from context alone.

Date and number answers show the highest context-following rates on negation. When a
negated sentence still contains a specific number (e.g., ``did not send 126 guns''), models
frequently extract the number despite the negation, producing answers that are faithful to
the surface form but semantically incorrect. This represents a systematic blind spot: the
presence of a numeric anchor overrides sensitivity to the negation operator.

\subsection{Paraphrase as a Diagnostic}

The paraphrase diagnostic confirms that surface wording affects outputs. The faithfulness
change ranges from $-0.034$ to $-0.051$ across models, and the EM change ranges from
$-0.026$ to $-0.099$. These values are smaller than those for entity swap and negation,
but they show that paraphrase is a control with measurable variation rather than a
perfectly invariant condition.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Entity-swap and negation failures are examined across all six models at $N=1{,}000$ (see
\Cref{app:error-analysis} for full category counts). Errors fall into
three categories: (a) the model answered correctly from parametric memory despite
corrupted context, (b) the model accepted the corrupted context and produced a wrong
answer, or (c) the model abstained.

\paragraph{Category (a): Correct answer despite corrupted context.}
The model outputs the gold answer even though the corrupted context does not support it.
This category can reflect parametric memory or an incomplete perturbation and therefore
is not treated as direct evidence of calibrated behavior.

\paragraph{Category (b): Followed corrupted context.}
The model produces an answer entailed by the wrong context and therefore incorrect. This
is the most actionable failure mode: the output is coherent and high-confidence, making
errors difficult to detect downstream.

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Category (b): Context-follower on entity swap}\\[4pt]
\textbf{Question:} What threat does the Global Adaptation Index study?\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perturbed context (entity swap):} As of 2013, the university is home to the Notre
Dame Global Adaptation Index which ranks countries 2010 based on how vulnerable they are
to climate change and how prepared they are to adapt.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model output (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}):} The Global Adaptation Index studies the
vulnerability of countries to climate change and their preparedness to adapt.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Faithfulness:} 0.99 \quad \textbf{EM:} \textit{false} \quad
\textbf{Perturbation:} entity swap\\[2pt]
\textit{The swapped entity left the claim structure intact; the model faithfully echoed
the corrupted context without detecting the substitution.}
\end{examplebox}

\paragraph{Category (c): Appropriate abstention on corrupted context.}

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Category (c): Correct abstention on negation}\\[4pt]
\textbf{Question:} In what year did Jerome Green send his first wireless message?\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perturbed context (negation):} Around 1899, Professor Jerome Green did not become
the first American to send a wireless message.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model output (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}):} I don't know.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Faithfulness:} 0.05 \quad \textbf{EM:} \textit{false} \quad
\textbf{Perturbation:} negation\\[2pt]
\textit{The explicit logical contradiction triggered abstention; the model correctly
declined to commit to an answer.}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Over-abstention as miscalibration.}
All six models abstain at non-trivial rates on clean original context (10--16\%). Given
the prompt's instruction to abstain only when context is insufficient, these responses
represent lost coverage on answerable inputs. Well-calibrated behavior requires low clean
context abstention together with higher abstention on corrupted context, rather than a
uniformly cautious response.

\paragraph{Closed-source models show stronger calibration, but the gap is narrow.}
The two closed-source models have the highest observed cliff scores, 0.241 and 0.244,
while open-weight scores range from 0.173 to 0.234. The margin is narrow: the difference
between the best open-weight and best closed-source model is 0.010. This six-model sample
does not establish a general closed-source advantage.

\paragraph{Model family over scale.}
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} (8B parameters) does not exceed its 7B competitors on the
abstention cliff despite achieving the highest baseline faithfulness (0.79).
Within this small model set, parameter count alone does not order abstention calibration.
The data are insufficient to identify whether model family, training data, alignment
method, or another design choice accounts for the ranking.

\paragraph{Implications for RAG system design.}
Model selection for RAG should assess clean-context coverage and response to corrupted
context together. The abstention cliff complements standard answer-quality metrics because
it captures their difference across retrieval conditions. The similarity-score result in
\Cref{sec:similarity} further shows that a lower descriptive calibration discrepancy does
not necessarily imply improved selective abstention.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Language.} All perturbations, models, and NLI scorers are English-only.
    Abstention calibration behavior may differ substantially across languages, particularly
    for models with non-English-centric training corpora such as \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}.

  \item \textbf{Entity swap coverage.} The lookup table covers common named entity types;
    rare or domain-specific entities (medical, legal, technical) may not have suitable
    replacements. Human validation confirmed that entity swap failures are concentrated in
    chemical compound names and other domain-specific terms outside the lookup table's
    scope.

  \item \textbf{Negation coverage.} The dependency-parse negation handles simple
    declarative sentences; complex, compound, or passive constructions may produce less
    natural negations, which remain in the evaluation set. Human validation identified
    multi-clause sentences as a systematic source of negation failures, where negating
    the root verb creates internal logical contradictions with dependent clauses.

  \item \textbf{Paraphrase quality.} The LLM paraphraser does not reliably preserve
    quoted speech verbatim, altering words attributed to named individuals. This is a
    known failure mode of instruction-tuned paraphrasers and is reflected in the
    human validation correctness scores.

  \item \textbf{Closed-source model opacity.} \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and
    \texttt{claude-haiku} are accessed through provider interfaces, so their training data, instruction-tuning
    methodology, or RLHF signal. The closed-source advantage in abstention calibration is
    an empirical observation; causal attribution to specific training factors is not
    possible without access to model internals.

  \item \textbf{Similarity score experiment scope.} The retrieval similarity score
    ablation is conducted on a 200-example subset of one model (\texttt{llama3.2:3b}).
    Whether the dissociation between global calibration (ECE) and selective calibration
    ($\Delta_\text{abstain}$) generalizes to other models or larger subsets remains an
    open question.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} All experiments use SQuAD v1.1, which consists of
    Wikipedia passages with extractive answers. Abstention behavior may differ on
    open-ended, multi-hop, or domain-specific QA tasks where the answer is not directly
    extractable from a single context sentence.

  \item \textbf{Single retriever.} All experiments use FAISS with
    \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}. Different retriever architectures or similarity functions
    may change retrieved passage distributions and affect results.

  \item \textbf{NLI scorer assumptions.} The DeBERTa-v3 NLI model is imperfectly
    calibrated for short factoid answers and may misclassify entailment in edge cases,
    particularly for numeric answers where a negated sentence still contains the correct
    number as a surface-form anchor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a counterfactual benchmark for measuring how RAG generators respond
to controlled retrieval failure. By holding the question, answer, and prompt fixed while
varying context content across original, paraphrase, entity-swap, and negation conditions,
the design isolates generator response to retrieval content across six models and 1{,}000
paired examples.

Faithfulness decreases by 0.153--0.295 on corrupted contexts, and abstention increases by
13.4--30.9 percentage points; every primary paired 95\% confidence interval excludes
zero. Hallucination is less frequent, with observed rates from no events to 9\%.
The two closed-source models have the largest observed abstention cliffs, while the Llama
models have the smallest. The matched similarity-score ablation reduces a descriptive
calibration discrepancy but also reduces the selective-abstention score. These findings
identify selective abstention as a measurable weakness under controlled retrieval failure.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Future evaluation should test additional QA datasets, multilingual contexts, more model
families, and larger matched similarity-score studies. Controlled training interventions
for selective abstention also require direct evaluation before claims about their efficacy
are warranted.

\section{Error Analysis Breakdown}
\label{app:error-analysis}

\Cref{tab:error-breakdown} reports the full error-analysis category counts across all six
models and both corrupted perturbation types ($N=1{,}000$ per cell, 6 models = 6{,}000
records per perturbation type).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Error analysis category counts (all six models combined, corrupted conditions
  only). Cat.\ (a): correct despite corrupted context (parametric memory, EM true).
  Cat.\ (b): followed corrupted context (context-follower, faithfulness $> 0.70$, EM
  false). Cat.\ (c): abstained on corrupted condition. ``Other'': wrong answer, low
  faithfulness, not abstained.}
\label{tab:error-breakdown}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Entity swap} & \textbf{\%} & \textbf{Negation} & \textbf{\%} \\
\midrule
(a) Parametric memory (correct) &   546 &  9.1\% &   627 & 10.5\% \\
(b) Context-follower (wrong)    & 2{,}780 & 46.3\% & 2{,}085 & 34.8\% \\
(c) Appropriate abstention      & 1{,}874 & 31.2\% & 2{,}399 & 40.0\% \\
Other (wrong, low faith)        &   800 & 13.3\% &   889 & 14.8\% \\
\midrule
Total                           & 6{,}000 & 100\% & 6{,}000 & 100\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Category (b) is the most actionable failure mode. Context-following on entity swap
(2,780 instances, 46.3\%) substantially exceeds context-following on negation (2,085
instances, 34.8\%), consistent with the finding that plausible wrong entities are harder
to detect than explicit logical contradictions. When a retrieved document contains a
structurally correct but factually wrong entity, models largely accept and echo the
corrupted content with high faithfulness and no signal of uncertainty.

Appropriate abstention (category c) is more common on negation (40.0\%) than entity swap
(31.2\%), reflecting the same asymmetry observed in the cliff score analysis: explicit
logical contradiction is a stronger abstention trigger than entity-level substitution.
The concern raised in \Cref{sec:rq3} is that these same models also abstain at
non-trivial rates on clean original context (10--16\%), meaning the category (c) counts
include both well-calibrated abstentions on corrupted input and over-abstentions that
would also occur on clean input.

Parametric memory override (category a) is more common on negation (10.5\%) than entity
swap (9.1\%). This is consistent with the hypothesis that negated sentences still contain
the correct factual anchor, such as a number, name, or date, which models retrieve from
parametric memory despite the negation operator, producing a technically correct answer
through the wrong mechanism.
