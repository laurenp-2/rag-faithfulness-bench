\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems are widely deployed, yet existing evaluation
frameworks measure only end-to-end answer quality and cannot isolate how generators behave
when retrieval fails. We introduce a counterfactual perturbation benchmark that
systematically corrupts retrieved context via three failure modes: entity substitution,
factual negation, and paraphrase (control). We evaluate four open-weight LLMs
(\texttt{llama3.2:3b}, \texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{mistral:7b}, \texttt{qwen2.5:7b})
and two closed-source models (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, \texttt{claude-haiku})
across 1{,}000 SQuAD v1.1 examples per condition ($N=24{,}000$ total).
Faithfulness drops significantly on corrupted conditions ($p < .001$) and abstention rises
across all models, though the abstention cliff is shallower than a well-calibrated model
should exhibit. Hallucination rates remain low (0--9\%) across all conditions; the
primary failure mode is indiscriminate refusal rather than confabulation. Closed-source
models achieve the strongest abstention calibration
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.24$), while the Llama family shows the flattest,
least condition-sensitive response ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.17$--$0.19$).
Supplying the retriever's similarity score as a prompt prefix reduces calibration error
(ECE) by 60\% but does not improve selective abstention, suggesting that global and
condition-sensitive calibration are distinct failure modes. Human validation of 80
perturbation examples confirms pipeline quality (mean fluency 2.73, mean correctness 2.48,
Cohen's $\kappa = 0.40$ for fluency and $\kappa = 0.48$ for correctness). Paraphrase and
original conditions show small but detectable surface-form effects at $N=1{,}000$, while
remaining far closer to each other than to corrupted conditions. We release the benchmark
as an open-source toolkit with full evaluation scripts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become the dominant architecture for grounding
large language model (LLM) outputs in external knowledge~\cite{lewis2020rag}. By coupling
a dense retriever with a generative model, RAG systems reduce hallucination and enable
up-to-date answers without retraining. They are now widely deployed in enterprise search,
legal document analysis, medical question answering, and customer-facing AI assistants.
Retrieval is inherently noisy: retrievers regularly surface stale documents,
near-duplicate passages with incorrect dates or entities, or topically adjacent but
factually mismatched chunks. Retrieval failure is not an edge case but a routine feature
of production RAG systems.

Existing evaluation frameworks measure only end-to-end answer quality. RAGAS~\cite{es2023ragas}
and ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2023ares} score faithfulness and answer relevance, but both
assume the retrieved context is the \emph{best available} context and treat errors as a
monolithic signal. When a RAG system gives a wrong answer, neither framework can determine
whether the retriever surfaced the wrong document or whether the generator failed to
reason correctly from a good one. TruLens and the TREC RAG Track~\cite{trec2024rag}
similarly measure pipeline outputs without isolating fault.

That attribution gap has a practical consequence: system designers cannot determine which
component to improve, and model developers lack benchmarks that test the
retrieval-generation interface under controlled failure conditions.

We address this gap with a counterfactual perturbation benchmark that systematically
corrupts retrieved context in three distinct ways and measures how LLM generators respond.
The design rests on a simple idea: if we \emph{know} the context is wrong because we made
it wrong, we can measure whether the model knows it too. We define four context conditions
for each QA pair:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Original}: unmodified gold context (baseline)
  \item \textbf{Paraphrase}: surface rewording with facts preserved (control)
  \item \textbf{Entity swap}: one named entity replaced with a plausible same-type alternative
  \item \textbf{Negation}: core factual claim directly contradicted via dependency-parse negation
\end{itemize}

The paraphrase condition serves as a control: a robust model should perform identically on
paraphrase and original. Meaningful divergence on entity swap and negation reveals failure
modes of practical interest.

This paper addresses four research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}]
  \item Do LLMs faithfully follow corrupted context, even when it is wrong?
  \item Do LLMs hallucinate when retrieved context conflicts with ground truth?
  \item Do LLMs appropriately abstain (``I don't know'') under corrupted retrieval?
  \item Does model scale, family, or open- vs.\ closed-source status predict better
        graceful degradation?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item A reproducible counterfactual RAG benchmark built on SQuAD
        v1.1~\cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, evaluated at scale ($N=1{,}000$ examples,
        $24{,}000$ total inferences) with full evaluation scripts released open-source.
  \item Empirical evaluation across six models spanning open-weight and closed-source
        families, yielding a $6 \times 4$ results matrix with full significance testing
        and answer-type cross-tabulation.
  \item A failure-mode taxonomy distinguishing three generator archetypes:
        \emph{context-follower}, \emph{hallucinator}, and \emph{over-abstainer}, with
        evidence that closed-source models exhibit stronger abstention calibration than
        open-weight models of comparable size.
  \item An ablation showing that supplying retrieval similarity scores as a prompt prefix
        improves global calibration (ECE $-60\%$) but not selective abstention, revealing
        a distinction between global and condition-sensitive calibration failure modes.
  \item Human validation of perturbation quality across 80 annotated examples with
        inter-rater agreement reporting.
  \item Practical guidance for RAG system designers on model selection and prompt
        engineering for calibrated abstention.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{RAG evaluation.}
RAGAS~\cite{es2023ragas} and ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2023ares} score faithfulness and answer
relevance using LLM judges, and the TREC RAG 2024 Track~\cite{trec2024rag} provides
large-scale IR-style evaluation, but all three frameworks evaluate the final answer and
the retrieved context together with no mechanism for holding retrieval quality constant.
Our benchmark holds retrieval quality constant by construction and varies only the
\emph{content} of the retrieved context in controlled ways, allowing us to attribute
failures to the generator rather than the retriever.

\paragraph{Robustness to adversarial context.}
Prior work on adversarial reading comprehension includes AddSent~\cite{jia2017adversarial},
which appends distracting sentences to SQuAD passages, contrast sets via minimal manual
perturbations~\cite{gardner2020contrast}, and counterfactual data
augmentation~\cite{kaushik2020counterfactual}. Our work targets a different problem: we
simulate realistic retrieval failure modes (plausible wrong entities, contradicting
documents) and measure abstention calibration, a behavior not studied by prior robustness
benchmarks.

\paragraph{Hallucination, abstention, and irrelevant context.}
TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa} measures false statements on misconception-prone
questions; calibration literature~\cite{kadavath2022language, xiong2024can} studies
whether model confidence correlates with accuracy; and selective
prediction~\cite{geifman2017selective} formalizes abstaining when confidence is low. Most
relevant to our setting, Shi et al.~\cite{shi2023large} show that irrelevant context can
mislead LLMs even when correct context is also present. We build on this by treating
abstention as a first-class metric under a controlled perturbation protocol and connecting
abstention rates to specific retrieval failure modes. CheckList~\cite{ribeiro2020checklist}
and ANLI~\cite{nie2020anli} establish the broader paradigm of behavioral testing via
perturbations; our benchmark applies similar logic but centers on abstention calibration
and production RAG failure modes.

\paragraph{Open-weight vs.\ closed-source model behavior.}
Recent work has begun systematically comparing open-weight and closed-source LLMs on
reliability and calibration tasks~\cite{kadavath2022language, xiong2024can}. Closed-source
models are generally found to express uncertainty more reliably, though the mechanisms
behind this difference — instruction-tuning methodology, RLHF signal, or training data
composition — remain poorly understood. Our benchmark provides a controlled setting for
this comparison: by holding the prompt, task, and evaluation protocol constant across
open-weight and closed-source models, we isolate abstention calibration as a measurable
axis of difference between model families.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\subsection{Seed Dataset}

We draw seed data from SQuAD v1.1~\cite{rajpurkar2016squad}, a reading comprehension
dataset of Wikipedia passages paired with crowd-sourced questions and extractive answers.
We apply the following filters to obtain clean, unambiguous examples:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item The gold context must be extractable as a single sentence containing the gold answer
        (the sentence is found by splitting on periods and selecting the first that
        includes the answer string verbatim; sentences shorter than 20 characters are skipped).
  \item The gold answer string must appear verbatim in the selected context sentence.
\end{itemize}

Filtering yields a dataset of 1{,}000 QA pairs, shuffled randomly from the full SQuAD
v1.1 training split. Each example has the format:
\begin{center}
\texttt{\{id, question, gold\_answer, gold\_context\}}
\end{center}
\Cref{tab:dataset-stats} reports dataset statistics.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Dataset statistics for the 1{,}000-example evaluation split.}
\label{tab:dataset-stats}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Total QA pairs                     & 1{,}000 \\
Source                             & SQuAD v1.1 (Wikipedia) \\
Avg.\ gold context length (tokens) & 27.2 \\
Avg.\ question length (tokens)     & 10.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Answer type labels (person, organization, location, date/number) were derived during
error analysis using spaCy NER applied to gold answers and are reported per-condition
in \Cref{sec:crosstab}; they were not stored in the base dataset.

\subsection{Perturbation Types}

For each QA pair we generate four context variants. \Cref{tab:perturbations} summarizes
the conditions.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Perturbation conditions. Each QA pair is evaluated under all four conditions.}
\label{tab:perturbations}
\begin{tabular}{llccp{3.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Transformation} & \textbf{Facts} & \textbf{Wording} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
Original    & None                        & $\checkmark$ & $\checkmark$ & Baseline / upper bound \\
Paraphrase  & LLM surface rewrite         & $\checkmark$ & $\times$     & Control: surface sensitivity \\
Entity swap & Same-type NER replacement   & $\times$     & $\checkmark$ & Simulates wrong-entity retrieval \\
Negation    & Dependency-parse negation   & $\times$     & $\checkmark$ & Simulates contradicting retrieval \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Original.} The unmodified gold context sentence. It sets the upper bound for
correctness and the expected lower bound for abstention: a well-calibrated model should
rarely decline to answer when the context is correct and directly relevant.

\paragraph{Paraphrase.} We prompt the generator LLM to rewrite the gold context sentence
while preserving all factual content. A robust model should produce near-identical metric
values on paraphrase and original. Meaningful divergence signals sensitivity to surface
wording rather than semantic content.

\paragraph{Entity swap.} We use spaCy's named entity recognizer to identify named
entities in the context, then select one at random from those whose label appears in
our swap table and replace it with a plausible same-type alternative drawn from a
curated lookup (e.g., swapping a PERSON entity for another prominent individual,
a GPE for another city or country). This condition simulates a retrieved document that
looks structurally correct but contains the wrong key fact.

\paragraph{Negation.} We parse the dependency tree of the gold context sentence,
identify the root verb of the main clause, and insert a negation via rule-based
transformation (inserting ``not'' for simple verbs, handling auxiliary constructions
appropriately). This simulates a retrieved document, such as a corrected record or updated
policy, that directly contradicts the original claim.

The following worked example shows all four conditions for the same QA pair:

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Question:} Who co-founded Microsoft?\\[4pt]
\textbf{Gold answer:} Bill Gates\\[4pt]
\textbf{Original:}    Microsoft was founded by Bill Gates and Paul Allen in 1975.\\
\textbf{Paraphrase:}  Bill Gates and Paul Allen established Microsoft in 1975.\\
\textbf{Entity swap:} Microsoft was founded by Steve Jobs and Paul Allen in 1975.\\
\textbf{Negation:}    Microsoft was not founded by Bill Gates and Paul Allen in 1975.
\end{examplebox}

\subsection{Perturbation Quality}

Automatic perturbation pipelines can produce fluency errors or incoherent outputs,
particularly for negation on complex sentences. The negation module applies a three-tier
fallback: auxiliary verb substitution (e.g., \textit{was} $\to$ \textit{was not}),
direct verb negation via lemma insertion (\textit{founded} $\to$ \textit{did not found}),
and a string-level fallback that inserts ``not'' after the first auxiliary keyword or
prepends ``It is not true that\ldots'' if none is found. The entity swap module
skips sentences for which no entity label in the swap table is present, returning the
original context unchanged.

To validate pipeline quality, two independent annotators rated 80 randomly sampled
examples (20 per condition) on two dimensions: fluency (1 = grammatically broken,
2 = slightly awkward, 3 = fluent and natural) and correctness (1 = perturbation failed,
2 = partial success, 3 = clear success). Inter-rater agreement was Cohen's
$\kappa = 0.396$ for fluency and $\kappa = 0.479$ for correctness. Mean fluency
across all conditions was 2.725 and mean correctness was 2.475.
Entity swap failures were concentrated in chemical compound and domain-specific entity
names not covered by the lookup table; paraphrase failures arose primarily from alteration
of quoted speech, where the LLM paraphraser did not preserve words inside quotation marks
verbatim. Negation failures were most common on multi-clause sentences where negating the
root verb created internal logical contradictions with dependent clauses. These failure
modes are consistent with known limitations of rule-based perturbation pipelines and are
reflected in the limitations discussion (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Evaluation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Retriever}

For each (QA pair, perturbation type) combination we build a FAISS \texttt{IndexFlatIP}
index over the single perturbed context string using
\texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} embeddings with normalized
vectors~\cite{reimers2019sentencebert}, retrieving $k=1$ passage at query time.
This design isolates generator behavior from retrieval noise: the model always receives
exactly the context produced by the perturbation, so observed metric differences across
conditions reflect generator response to content changes rather than retrieval variation.

\subsection{Generator}

We evaluate six models spanning open-weight and closed-source families. Open-weight models
are served locally via Ollama for full reproducibility; closed-source models are accessed
via API at temperature 0 using the same prompt template.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \texttt{llama3.2:3b} (3B parameters): smallest open-weight model, serves as baseline
  \item \texttt{llama3.1:8b} (8B parameters): same family at larger scale
  \item \texttt{mistral:7b}  (7B parameters): different instruction-tuning lineage
  \item \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}  (7B parameters): non-English-centric training corpus
  \item \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}: closed-source, OpenAI instruction-tuning lineage; accessed via OpenAI API
  \item \texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}: closed-source, Anthropic instruction-tuning lineage; accessed via Anthropic API
\end{itemize}

All models are prompted at temperature 0 for reproducibility using a system prompt and
a structured user message:

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{System:} You are a precise question-answering assistant. Answer the question
using ONLY the information provided in the context. If the context does not contain
enough information to answer the question, respond with exactly: I don't know. Do not
add information from your own knowledge. Keep your answer concise --- one sentence or
less.\\[6pt]
\textbf{User:}\\
\texttt{Context:}\\
\texttt{[1] \{retrieved\_context\}}\\[4pt]
\texttt{Question: \{question\}}\\[4pt]
\texttt{Answer:}
\end{examplebox}

\noindent The identical prompt is used for all six models to ensure that observed
differences in abstention and faithfulness reflect model behavior rather than
prompt sensitivity.

\subsection{Metrics}

\Cref{tab:metrics} defines all six evaluation metrics.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Evaluation metrics, operationalization, and ideal direction on corrupted conditions.}
\label{tab:metrics}
\begin{tabular}{p{2.8cm}p{4.5cm}p{2cm}p{2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Operationalization} & \textbf{Tool} & \textbf{Ideal (corrupted)} \\
\midrule
Faithfulness       & NLI entailment: $P(\text{answer} \mid \text{context})$ & DeBERTa-v3 NLI & Low \\
Exact match (EM)   & Normalized string match with gold answer    & String      & Low \\
Token F1           & Token-level F1 with gold answer             & String      & Low \\
Hallucination      & NLI contradiction (answer vs.\ context) \textbf{and} token F1 $< 0.2$ \textbf{and} NLI contradiction (generated answer vs.\ gold answer) & DeBERTa-v3 & Low (all) \\
Abstention rate    & Output begins with ``I don't know'' (prefix match, case-insensitive) & String & High \\
Contradiction rate & NLI contradiction: answer vs.\ context      & DeBERTa-v3 & Moderate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The faithfulness score is computed as:
\begin{equation}
  \text{faithfulness}(a, c) = P_\text{NLI}(\text{entailment} \mid \text{premise}=c,\;
  \text{hypothesis}=a)
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the generated answer and $c$ is the perturbed retrieved context, scored by
\texttt{cross-encoder/nli-\allowbreak deberta-v3-base}.

We additionally define an \emph{abstention cliff score} to summarize each model's
condition-sensitivity in a single value:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_\text{abstain} = \frac{1}{2}
    \bigl[\text{Abstain}(\text{entity\_swap}) + \text{Abstain}(\text{negation})\bigr]
    - \text{Abstain}(\text{original})
  \label{eq:cliff}
\end{equation}
A higher $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ indicates that a model abstains more on corrupted
conditions relative to clean context — the desired behavior for a well-calibrated RAG
generator. An ideal model would score near $+0.70$, abstaining on nearly all corrupted
inputs while committing on nearly all clean ones.

We also report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) to measure global alignment between
faithfulness and abstention rate across binned faithfulness intervals. ECE complements
$\Delta_\text{abstain}$: a model can improve ECE by becoming uniformly more cautious
without improving condition-sensitivity, as we demonstrate in the similarity score
ablation (\Cref{sec:similarity}).

\subsection{Statistical Testing}

We use the following tests to assess whether differences across conditions are
statistically reliable:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Two-proportion $z$-test} for pairwise condition comparisons on
        proportion-valued metrics (EM, abstention, hallucination, contradiction).
  \item \textbf{Cohen's $h$} as an effect size measure for proportion comparisons.
  \item \textbf{Bonferroni correction} for multiple comparisons across conditions and
        metrics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ1: Do Models Follow Corrupted Context?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Faithfulness drops significantly from original to corrupted conditions across all six
models. \texttt{llama3.1:8b} achieves the highest baseline faithfulness (0.79) and drops
to 0.62 on entity swap ($p < .001$) and 0.55 on negation ($p < .001$).
\texttt{mistral:7b} starts lower (0.67) and falls most steeply to 0.40 on negation
($p < .001$), suggesting it is less inclined to follow context under contradiction. Among
closed-source models, \texttt{claude-haiku} shows the lowest baseline EM (0.06) and F1
(0.32) on original context, indicating a more conservative answering style even on clean
input.

At $N=1{,}000$, paraphrase faithfulness differences reach statistical significance for
three models (\texttt{llama3.1:8b}: $p=.005$; \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}: $p=.011$;
\texttt{claude-haiku}: $p=.023$), a pattern not detectable at smaller sample sizes.
However, effect sizes remain small (faith $\Delta$ range: $-0.034$ to $-0.051$) and fall
well below the drops observed on entity swap and negation. EM differences are significant
for most models but similarly modest in magnitude. We interpret these as minor surface-form
sensitivity rather than substantive faithfulness failures: the paraphrase pipeline
introduces slight wording differences that affect extractive EM without meaningfully
changing whether the model follows context. The core finding that entity swap and negation
produce far larger drops remains robust. Full paraphrase diagnostics are reported in
\Cref{tab:paraphrase-diagnostic}.

\subsection{RQ2: Do Models Hallucinate?}
\label{sec:rq2}

Hallucination rates remain low across all six models and conditions, ranging from 0\% to
9\%. Entity swap consistently produces the highest hallucination rates: \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}
peaks at 9\% on entity swap, and \texttt{llama3.1:8b} and \texttt{mistral:7b} each reach
7\%. A plausible-but-wrong entity is more likely to elicit confabulation than an explicit
negation, where the syntactic contradiction is overt. Negation hallucination rates are
statistically indistinguishable from baseline for most models.

Notably, \texttt{claude-haiku} shows near-zero hallucination across all conditions
(0--2\%), the lowest of any model evaluated. This may reflect more conservative decoding
behavior or stronger instruction-following that suppresses fabrication even when context
is corrupted. The primary failure mode under corrupted retrieval remains over-abstention
rather than confabulation across all six models.

\subsection{RQ3: Is Abstention Calibrated?}
\label{sec:rq3}

The abstention results are the central finding. A well-calibrated model would show low
abstention on original and paraphrase conditions and high abstention on corrupted
conditions. Observed cliffs are real but shallower than ideal across all six models.

The Llama family shows the weakest calibration. \texttt{llama3.2:3b} abstains at only
14\% on correct original context and reaches 30\% on entity swap and 32\% on negation
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.173$, $p < .001$). \texttt{llama3.1:8b} shows a similarly
flat response (baseline 10\%, rising to 28\% and 31\% on corrupted conditions,
$\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.192$). Both models commit readily on clean context but fail
to substantially increase abstention under corruption.

Among open-weight models, \texttt{mistral:7b} and \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} show stronger
calibration. \texttt{mistral:7b} rises from 13\% baseline to 43\% on negation, a 30
percentage-point increase, though entity swap abstention rises only modestly to 26\%,
reflecting the asymmetric failure mode described in \Cref{sec:mistral}.
\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} shows a more balanced response: 37\% on entity swap and 43\% on
negation ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.234$).

Closed-source models achieve the strongest abstention cliffs. \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} rises
from 14\% baseline to 32\% on entity swap and 44\% on negation
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.241$). \texttt{claude-haiku} rises from 16\% to 35\% and
46\% respectively ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.244$), the highest of any model evaluated.
Both closed-source models show more balanced detection across entity swap and negation
than their open-weight counterparts.

\subsection{Retrieval Similarity Score as a Calibration Intervention}
\label{sec:similarity}

The results above raise a practical question: can a simple prompt-level intervention
improve abstention calibration without fine-tuning? We test this by supplying the FAISS
retrieval similarity score as a prefix to the context field, allowing the model to
condition its confidence explicitly on retrieval quality:

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Context (with similarity prefix):}\\
\texttt{Retrieval confidence: 0.43 (low)}\\
\texttt{[1] \{retrieved\_context\}}
\end{examplebox}

We run this intervention on \texttt{llama3.2:3b} over a 200-example subset (800 total
inferences). Results are summarized in \Cref{tab:similarity-ablation}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Similarity score intervention ablation on \texttt{llama3.2:3b} ($n=200$
  examples). ECE computed over faithfulness bins; $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ as defined
  in \Cref{eq:cliff}.}
\label{tab:similarity-ablation}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{ECE} & $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ & \textbf{Orig.\ Abstain} \\
\midrule

The similarity score intervention reduces ECE by 60\% (0.1280 $\to$ 0.0516), a
substantial improvement in global calibration. However, $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ decreases
from $+0.173$ to $+0.135$. The intervention raises baseline abstention on clean original
context (13.8\% $\to$ 23.0\%) almost as much as it raises abstention on corrupted
conditions, making the model more uniformly cautious rather than more condition-sensitive.

This dissociation reveals that global calibration (ECE) and selective calibration
($\Delta_\text{abstain}$) are distinct failure modes. A retrieval confidence signal helps
the model recognize uncertainty in aggregate but does not teach it to discriminate between
clean and corrupted context specifically. Contrastive fine-tuning on paired
clean/corrupted examples remains the more promising path to condition-sensitive abstention.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Failure Mode Taxonomy}

Drawing on the joint distribution of faithfulness, EM, hallucination, and abstention, we
identify three generator archetypes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Context-follower.} High faithfulness on corrupted conditions, low EM. The
    model accepts the retrieved context even when it is wrong and produces answers
    consistent with the incorrect passage. Errors of this type are difficult to detect in
    production because the outputs are coherent and internally consistent. No model in our
    evaluation is a pure context-follower, but all show partial context-following on entity
    swap (faithfulness 0.51--0.62 on corrupted conditions across open-weight models).

  \item \textbf{Hallucinator.} Low faithfulness, low EM, elevated hallucination rate. The
    model disregards the retrieved context and produces a plausible-sounding answer from
    parametric memory. This archetype is mostly absent from our results: hallucination
    peaks at 9\% and is rarely significant, indicating these models do not systematically
    fabricate answers under retrieval pressure. \texttt{claude-haiku} shows the strongest
    resistance to this failure mode, with hallucination rates of 0--2\% across all
    conditions.

  \item \textbf{Over-abstainer.} High uniform abstention regardless of context quality.
    The model declines to answer at similar rates on clean and corrupted context alike.
    All six models show some degree of over-abstainer behavior. The Llama family shows it
    most clearly: \texttt{llama3.2:3b} abstains at 14\% on correct original context and
    produces a cliff score of only $\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.173$, the weakest of any
    model evaluated.
\end{itemize}

An ideal model would commit on clean context and abstain on corrupted context, producing a
cliff score near $+0.70$ or higher. All six models fall short, with scores ranging from
$+0.173$ to $+0.244$. Closed-source models approach the upper bound of this range but
remain far from ideal, indicating that the over-abstainer failure mode is systemic across
current LLM families rather than specific to open-weight models.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/fig4_taxonomy_scatter.pdf}
\caption{Failure mode taxonomy. Each point is a (model, condition) pair plotted by
  faithfulness rate ($x$) and abstention rate ($y$) across all six models. Shaded
  regions mark the three archetypes. Corrupted conditions (entity swap, negation) move
  left and up relative to original/paraphrase, but most remain in the over-abstainer
  region rather than the ideal (low faithfulness, high abstention) region.}
\label{fig:taxonomy}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Mistral's Asymmetric Response to Failure Modes}
\label{sec:mistral}

\texttt{mistral:7b} shows an asymmetric response across corruption types. It abstains at
only 26\% on entity swap (barely above its 13\% baseline) but rises to 43\% on negation,
a 30 percentage-point increase. Mistral is particularly sensitive to explicit logical
contradiction but does not reliably detect entity-level substitutions. In practice, this
means Mistral responds well when retrieval errors produce direct factual contradictions
(e.g., a corrected policy document) but less well when errors involve plausible wrong
entities that do not generate an obvious logical conflict.

\texttt{qwen2.5:7b} shows a more balanced response: 37\% on entity swap and 43\% on
negation, both significant at $p < .001$. More uniform detection across failure modes
makes Qwen the most robustly calibrated open-weight model in our evaluation, though both
closed-source models achieve higher overall cliff scores with similarly balanced
entity-swap and negation detection.

\subsection{Answer-Type Cross-Tabulation}
\label{sec:crosstab}

The expanded dataset enables analysis of failure mode distributions across answer types.
Drawing on the error analysis output across all six models, three patterns emerge.

Organization-type answers show the highest hallucination rates on entity swap across
models. This is consistent with the hypothesis that organization names are well-represented
in parametric memory: when a retrieved org name is wrong, models are more likely to
substitute a known alternative from training data rather than abstaining or following the
corrupted context.

Location-type answers show the lowest abstention rates on entity swap despite
corruption — models are more likely to accept and echo a plausible wrong location than to
abstain. A wrong city or country name is structurally indistinguishable from a correct one
without world knowledge, making location swaps the hardest failure mode for models to
detect from context alone.

Date and number answers show the highest context-following rates on negation. When a
negated sentence still contains a specific number (e.g., ``did not send 126 guns''), models
frequently extract the number despite the negation, producing answers that are faithful to
the surface form but semantically incorrect. This represents a systematic blind spot: the
presence of a numeric anchor overrides sensitivity to the negation operator.

\subsection{Paraphrase as a Diagnostic}

The paraphrase diagnostic results at $N=1{,}000$ reveal a more nuanced picture than the
original $N=150$ analysis. Faithfulness differences between paraphrase and original reach
significance for three models (\texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini},
\texttt{claude-haiku}), while EM differences are significant for most models. However,
effect sizes remain small across all six models (faith $\Delta$ range: $-0.034$ to
$-0.051$), well below the drops observed under entity swap and negation. These small but
detectable differences at larger sample sizes suggest that the paraphrase pipeline
introduces minor surface-form sensitivity — primarily through conservative phrasing that
slightly reduces extractive EM — without meaningfully undermining the core experimental
design. The semantic corruption signal from entity swap and negation remains clearly
distinguishable from paraphrase-level noise.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

We examine entity swap and negation failures systematically across all six models at
$N=1{,}000$ (see \Cref{app:error-analysis} for full category counts). Errors fall into
three categories: (a) the model answered correctly from parametric memory despite
corrupted context, (b) the model accepted the corrupted context and produced a wrong
answer, or (c) the model abstained.

\paragraph{Category (a): Correct answer despite corrupted context.}
The model ignores the wrong context and draws on parametric memory.

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Category (a) --- Parametric memory overrides corrupted context}\\[4pt]
\textbf{Question:} In what year was the Theodore M.\ Hesburgh Library at Notre Dame
finished?\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perturbed context (negation):} Hesburgh Library, completed in 1963, which is not
the third building to house the main collection of books.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model output (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}):} 1963\\[3pt]
\textbf{Faithfulness:} 0.02 \quad \textbf{EM:} \textit{true} \quad
\textbf{Perturbation:} negation\\[2pt]
\textit{The model correctly identifies 1963 despite the negated context, relying on
parametric knowledge. This is technically correct behavior but highlights that models
may appear calibrated when parametric memory compensates for retrieval failure.}
\end{examplebox}

\paragraph{Category (b): Followed corrupted context.}
The model produces an answer entailed by the wrong context and therefore incorrect. This
is the most actionable failure mode: the output is coherent and high-confidence, making
errors difficult to detect downstream.

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Category (b) --- Context-follower on entity swap}\\[4pt]
\textbf{Question:} What threat does the Global Adaptation Index study?\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perturbed context (entity swap):} As of 2013, the university is home to the Notre
Dame Global Adaptation Index which ranks countries 2010 based on how vulnerable they are
to climate change and how prepared they are to adapt.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model output (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}):} The Global Adaptation Index studies the
vulnerability of countries to climate change and their preparedness to adapt.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Faithfulness:} 1.00 \quad \textbf{EM:} \textit{false} \quad
\textbf{Perturbation:} entity swap\\[2pt]
\textit{The swapped entity left the claim structure intact; the model faithfully echoed
the corrupted context without detecting the substitution.}
\end{examplebox}

\paragraph{Category (c): Appropriate abstention on corrupted context.}

\begin{examplebox}
\small
\textbf{Category (c) --- Correct abstention on negation}\\[4pt]
\textbf{Question:} In what year did Jerome Green send his first wireless message?\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perturbed context (negation):} Around 1899, Professor Jerome Green did not become
the first American to send a wireless message.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model output (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b}):} I don't know.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Faithfulness:} 0.05 \quad \textbf{EM:} \textit{false} \quad
\textbf{Perturbation:} negation\\[2pt]
\textit{The explicit logical contradiction triggered abstention; the model correctly
declined to commit to an answer.}
\end{examplebox}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Over-abstention as miscalibration.}
All six models abstain at non-trivial rates on clean original context (10--16\%), though
notably lower than the 23--29\% observed in our initial 150-example pilot. This reduction
at larger sample size suggests the pilot may have overestimated baseline abstention, and
that models are somewhat more willing to commit on clean context than initially measured.
Nevertheless, abstaining on 10--16\% of correctly-retrieved questions is not appropriate
caution — it indicates that models treat abstention as a general-purpose response to
uncertainty rather than a targeted response to retrieval failure. In production RAG
deployments, declining to answer one in ten questions with correct context imposes real
costs on user experience and operator trust. Well-calibrated behavior means committing
when context is reliable and abstaining when it is not, rather than applying a uniform
threshold.

\paragraph{Closed-source models show stronger calibration, but the gap is narrow.}
The most striking finding in the expanded evaluation is that both closed-source models
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, \texttt{claude-haiku}) outperform all four open-weight models on
$\Delta_\text{abstain}$, occupying the top two positions in the ranking. However, the
margin is narrow: closed-source models score $+0.241$--$+0.244$ against open-weight scores
of $+0.173$--$+0.234$. The gap between the best open-weight model (\texttt{qwen2.5:7b},
$+0.234$) and the best closed-source model (\texttt{claude-haiku}, $+0.244$) is smaller
than the gap between the two Llama models ($+0.173$ vs.\ $+0.192$). This suggests that
the closed-source advantage in abstention calibration is real but not large, and that
model family and instruction-tuning methodology are more decisive than the open/closed
distinction per se.

\paragraph{Model family over scale.}
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} (8B parameters) does not outperform its 7B competitors on
calibration despite achieving the highest baseline faithfulness (0.79). Parameter count
is a weak predictor of abstention calibration. The Llama family clusters at the bottom
of the cliff score ranking regardless of size, while Mistral and Qwen outperform them at
the same parameter count. Instruction-tuning methodology and training data composition
are more plausible explanatory factors: \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}, which uses a multilingual
and instruction-diverse training corpus, achieves the most balanced open-weight response
across entity swap and negation conditions.

\paragraph{Implications for RAG system design.}
Model selection for RAG deployments should include $\Delta_\text{abstain}$ as a criterion
alongside standard accuracy metrics. Among open-weight models, \texttt{qwen2.5:7b} offers
the best combination of competitive baseline accuracy and symmetric calibration across
failure modes. Among all models tested, \texttt{claude-haiku} achieves the highest cliff
score and near-zero hallucination, making it the strongest option where API access is
acceptable. The similarity score ablation (\Cref{sec:similarity}) shows that supplying
the retriever's FAISS similarity score as a prompt prefix improves global calibration
(ECE $-60\%$) but does not improve selective abstention, distinguishing two failure modes
that require different remediation strategies: global miscalibration can be partially
addressed through prompt engineering, while condition-sensitive miscalibration likely
requires targeted fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Relationship to instruction tuning and RLHF.}
Abstention behavior is likely downstream of instruction tuning and reinforcement learning
from human feedback. Models trained to be uniformly cautious may over-abstain because the
training signal does not distinguish between low-quality and high-quality retrieved
context. The closed-source advantage in cliff scores is consistent with this hypothesis:
proprietary RLHF pipelines may include more nuanced feedback signals that reward
condition-sensitive abstention rather than uniform caution. Fine-tuning on contrastive
abstention examples --- correct abstentions on corrupted context paired with correct
commitments on clean context --- is a promising direction for improving calibration in
open-weight models without sacrificing accuracy on answerable questions.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Language.} All perturbations, models, and NLI scorers are English-only.
    Abstention calibration behavior may differ substantially across languages, particularly
    for models with non-English-centric training corpora such as \texttt{qwen2.5:7b}.

  \item \textbf{Entity swap coverage.} The lookup table covers common named entity types;
    rare or domain-specific entities (medical, legal, technical) may not have suitable
    replacements. Human validation confirmed that entity swap failures are concentrated in
    chemical compound names and other domain-specific terms outside the lookup table's
    scope.

  \item \textbf{Negation coverage.} The dependency-parse negation handles simple
    declarative sentences; complex, compound, or passive constructions may produce less
    natural negations, which remain in the evaluation set. Human validation identified
    multi-clause sentences as a systematic source of negation failures, where negating
    the root verb creates internal logical contradictions with dependent clauses.

  \item \textbf{Paraphrase quality.} The LLM paraphraser does not reliably preserve
    quoted speech verbatim, altering words attributed to named individuals. This is a
    known failure mode of instruction-tuned paraphrasers and is reflected in the
    human validation correctness scores.

  \item \textbf{Closed-source model opacity.} We evaluate \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and
    \texttt{claude-haiku} via API but have no access to their training data, instruction-tuning
    methodology, or RLHF signal. The closed-source advantage in abstention calibration is
    an empirical observation; causal attribution to specific training factors is not
    possible without access to model internals.

  \item \textbf{Similarity score experiment scope.} The retrieval similarity score
    ablation is conducted on a 200-example subset of one model (\texttt{llama3.2:3b}).
    Whether the dissociation between global calibration (ECE) and selective calibration
    ($\Delta_\text{abstain}$) generalizes to other models or larger subsets remains an
    open question.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} All experiments use SQuAD v1.1, which consists of
    Wikipedia passages with extractive answers. Abstention behavior may differ on
    open-ended, multi-hop, or domain-specific QA tasks where the answer is not directly
    extractable from a single context sentence.

  \item \textbf{Single retriever.} All experiments use FAISS with
    \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}. Different retriever architectures or similarity functions
    may change retrieved passage distributions and affect results.

  \item \textbf{NLI scorer assumptions.} The DeBERTa-v3 NLI model is imperfectly
    calibrated for short factoid answers and may misclassify entailment in edge cases,
    particularly for numeric answers where a negated sentence still contains the correct
    number as a surface-form anchor.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced a counterfactual perturbation benchmark for measuring how RAG pipeline
generators respond to controlled retrieval failure. By holding retrieval architecture
constant and varying only context content across four conditions (original, paraphrase,
entity swap, and negation), we isolate generator behavior from retrieval quality and
measure graceful degradation directly across six models spanning open-weight and
closed-source families at $N=1{,}000$ examples.

Faithfulness drops significantly on corrupted conditions ($p < .001$ in all cases) and
abstention rises with medium-to-large effect sizes ($h = 0.42$--$0.57$). Hallucination
rates remain low (0--9\%), identifying over-abstention rather than confabulation as the
primary failure mode across all six models. Closed-source models
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, \texttt{claude-haiku}) achieve the strongest abstention cliffs
($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.241$--$+0.244$), while the Llama family shows the flattest,
least condition-sensitive responses ($\Delta_\text{abstain} = +0.173$--$+0.192$). Scale
does not reliably predict calibration: \texttt{llama3.1:8b} (8B parameters) scores lower
than all three 7B competitors on $\Delta_\text{abstain}$. A retrieval similarity score
intervention reduces global calibration error (ECE) by 60\% but does not improve
selective abstention, revealing that global and condition-sensitive calibration are
distinct failure modes requiring different remediation strategies. Human validation of 80
perturbation examples confirms pipeline quality and characterizes systematic failure modes
in entity swap and negation generation.

We release the benchmark code, dataset, and evaluation scripts as an open-source toolkit
with full evaluation scripts. Future directions include extending to additional QA datasets
(TriviaQA, HotpotQA), evaluating larger closed-source models (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet),
exploring contrastive fine-tuning for improved condition-sensitive abstention, and
conducting multi-model similarity score ablations to determine whether the global
vs.\ selective calibration dissociation generalizes across model families.

\section{Error Analysis Breakdown}
\label{app:error-analysis}

\Cref{tab:error-breakdown} reports the full error-analysis category counts across all six
models and both corrupted perturbation types ($N=1{,}000$ per cell, 6 models = 6{,}000
records per perturbation type).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Error analysis category counts (all six models combined, corrupted conditions
  only). Cat.\ (a): correct despite corrupted context (parametric memory, EM true).
  Cat.\ (b): followed corrupted context (context-follower, faithfulness $> 0.70$, EM
  false). Cat.\ (c): abstained on corrupted condition. ``Other'': wrong answer, low
  faithfulness, not abstained.}
\label{tab:error-breakdown}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Entity swap} & \textbf{\%} & \textbf{Negation} & \textbf{\%} \\
\midrule
(a) Parametric memory (correct) &   546 &  9.1\% &   627 & 10.5\% \\
(b) Context-follower (wrong)    & 2{,}780 & 46.3\% & 2{,}085 & 34.8\% \\
(c) Appropriate abstention      & 1{,}874 & 31.2\% & 2{,}399 & 40.0\% \\
Other (wrong, low faith)        &   800 & 13.3\% &   889 & 14.8\% \\
\midrule
Total                           & 6{,}000 & 100\% & 6{,}000 & 100\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Category (b) is the most actionable failure mode. Context-following on entity swap
(2,780 instances, 46.3\%) substantially exceeds context-following on negation (2,085
instances, 34.8\%), consistent with the finding that plausible wrong entities are harder
to detect than explicit logical contradictions. When a retrieved document contains a
structurally correct but factually wrong entity, models largely accept and echo the
corrupted content with high faithfulness and no signal of uncertainty.

Appropriate abstention (category c) is more common on negation (40.0\%) than entity swap
(31.2\%), reflecting the same asymmetry observed in the cliff score analysis: explicit
logical contradiction is a stronger abstention trigger than entity-level substitution.
The concern raised in \Cref{sec:rq3} is that these same models also abstain at
non-trivial rates on clean original context (10--16\%), meaning the category (c) counts
include both well-calibrated abstentions on corrupted input and over-abstentions that
would also occur on clean input.

Parametric memory override (category a) is more common on negation (10.5\%) than entity
swap (9.1\%). This is consistent with the hypothesis that negated sentences still contain
the correct factual anchor --- a number, name, or date --- which models retrieve from
parametric memory despite the negation operator, producing a technically correct answer
through the wrong mechanism.
